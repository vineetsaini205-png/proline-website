## How a solar EV charging station works

Energy passes through five parts on its way from the sun to the vehicle:

1. **Panels** on a carport over the parking, or on a roof nearby, turn sunlight into DC power.
2. **The inverter** turns it into AC for your site. An on-grid inverter works alongside the grid; a hybrid inverter can also charge and use a battery.
3. **Battery storage,** if you add it, keeps surplus midday energy for the evening or for a power cut. See [BESS for charging stations](/batteries/bess-for-ev-charging/).
4. **The chargers:** AC for vehicles that park for hours, DC for quick stops. They use solar power first, and the grid or the battery fills any gap.
5. **The vehicle** charges as usual. The driver plugs in the same way, whatever the source.

In most designs the grid stays connected. When the sun is strong, surplus power runs your other loads, fills the battery or goes to the grid; when it is weak, the grid makes up the difference. No driver waits for the sun.

- **On-grid:** the simplest. There is no battery, and the system switches off in a power cut by design, so it never feeds a line that workers are repairing.
- **Hybrid:** adds a battery for evening charging or backup. Backup works only if the system is designed for it.
- **Off-grid:** for sites with no grid connection. It needs a large battery and suits light loads, such as 2W and e-rickshaw charging, far better than DC fast charging.

## Why daytime charging suits solar

Solar output peaks around midday, and many vehicles are parked then: staff cars at an office, delivery and cab fleets between shifts, buses at a depot. Every unit they take from the panels is a unit you don't buy.

The tariff points the same way. For public charging, the Ministry of Power's 2024 guidelines suggest a single-part tariff capped at the average cost of supply until 31 March 2028, with solar hours 30% cheaper (0.7 times) and non-solar hours 30% dearer (1.3 times) ([Lok Sabha answer, Aug 2025](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/2994845/1/AU3099_jxi0H3.pdf)). So a unit bought from the grid in the evening can cost nearly twice what the same unit costs at midday (1.3 ÷ 0.7). Your state's electricity regulatory commission sets the actual rates and hours in its tariff order.

For most sites the plan is simple: charge as much as you can in the day, from your own panels and the cheaper grid power, and add storage only if a lot of your charging happens in the evening.

## How much solar you need: a worked example

A commonly used rule of thumb in India is 4 to 5 units (kWh) a day for each kWp of panels. MNRE's rooftop solar FAQ puts a clear sunny day at 4 to 5.5 units per kWp ([MNRE](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2022/apr/doc202242850101.pdf)). Output varies by location and season: it is lower in the monsoon, on foggy winter days in North India, and when panels are shaded or dusty.

Take an office where 10 electric cars each need about 10 kWh on a working day.

| Step | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Energy the cars need | 10 cars × 10 kWh | About 100 units a day |
| Solar to match it | 100 ÷ 4 to 5 units per kWp | About 20 to 25 kWp |
| Space for it | Roughly 2 to 2.5 kWp over each car bay | A carport over the same 10 bays |
| What it makes | 20 to 25 kWp × 4 to 5 units | Roughly 80 to 125 units on a clear day |

On a clear day, the panels over the parking make about what the parked cars need. On cloudy days the grid makes up the difference, and at weekends the surplus runs the building or, with net metering, goes to the grid. AC chargers lose only a few per cent of the energy, so the plan holds.

DC fast chargers are a different story. A 60 kW charger busy for three hours a day uses about 180 units, while a 30 kWp canopy over its bays makes 120 to 150 units on a clear day. At a highway site, solar cuts the energy bill and shades the bays, and the grid, sometimes with a battery, carries the main load.

## Carport or rooftop

- **Solar carport.** It shades the parking and the vehicles, puts the panels right where the chargers are, and keeps your roof free. It needs foundations, a steel structure designed for the wind at your site, enough height for the tallest vehicles, and drainage. See [civil work](/services/ev-charging-civil-work/).
- **Rooftop.** It uses a roof you already have, with no foundations in the parking, and works best when the roof is close to the chargers, so the cable run stays short. The roof's strength, shade and waterproofing are checked first.
- **Both.** Larger sites often combine a carport over the charging bays with panels on nearby roofs.

Sending surplus power to the grid needs your DISCOM's approval for solar, such as net metering, and the rules differ by state. You apply to your DISCOM, and we tell you what the application needs.

## Where solar EV charging suits

- **Offices and campuses.** Staff cars park all day in solar hours, the best match of all.
- **Fleets and depots.** Delivery 2-wheelers and 3-wheelers, cabs and buses that charge between shifts or at midday. See [fleets](/for/fleets/).
- **Highway sites and fuel stations.** Solar lowers the energy bill and shades the bays. DC chargers still rely on the grid, and a battery can carry midday solar into the evening.
- **Homes.** Rooftop solar covers the house in the day, but most cars charge at night, so net metering or a battery bridges the gap. See [inverter and solar batteries](/batteries/inverter-solar-batteries/).
