## What you need to start an EV charging station

- **A site** where EVs already travel or park, which you own, lease or share revenue on.
- **Chargers** that match those vehicles and how long they stay: AC, DC or light-EV chargers.
- **An electricity connection** with its own meter, on your state's EV tariff, big enough for your chargers.
- **Software** to start sessions and take payment: an app platform or your own system.
- **A plan that pays** at realistic usage, without counting on a subsidy.

You don't need an electricity licence. Under the Ministry of Power's 2024 guidelines, setting up and running a charging station is a de-licensed activity, open to any individual or company ([Mercom, Sep 2024](https://www.mercomindia.com/ministry-of-power-releases-ev-charging-infrastructure-guidelines)). You still need the connection, the right to use the land and any local permissions your site needs.

## Is an EV charging station profitable?

It can be, at the right site. A station earns on the gap between what drivers pay for each unit of energy and what that unit costs you, multiplied by the number of units you sell. Rent, upkeep and the money you invested all have to come out of that gap.

The prices on both sides are public. Drivers pay about ₹8–15 per kWh at public AC chargers and ₹15–25 per kWh at DC chargers ([Statiq, Oct 2026](https://statiq.in/blog/2026/10/12/public-ev-charging-cost-india/)). On the cost side, the LT EV tariff in the states we work in runs from ₹4.50 a unit in Delhi to ₹7.70 in Uttar Pradesh (Tamil Nadu's varies from ₹6 to ₹9 a unit by time of day), and some states add a fixed charge for every kW of load ([BEE state-wise EV tariffs](https://beeindia.gov.in/sites/default/files/annexure-A.pdf)).

What decides profit is utilisation: how many hours a day vehicles are actually charging. An idle charger earns nothing but still costs fixed charges, rent and upkeep. Usage at a new station typically builds over 6–12 months ([Bolt.Earth](https://bolt.earth/franchise)), so keep cash aside for quiet early months.

Out of what drivers pay, you meet:

- **Electricity:** the EV tariff for every unit, the energy lost inside the charger, and any fixed charge per kW.
- **The site:** rent, or a share of revenue to the landowner.
- **Software:** an app platform's commission, or the cost of running your own system.
- **Running costs:** internet, cleaning, security, maintenance and, on larger sites, staff.

Work out your own numbers in the [earnings calculator](/tools/ev-charging-station-earnings-calculator/): choose your state, chargers and price, and it shows a quiet, likely and busy month, with the payback once you enter your own quote. Our article on [what a charging station earns](/blog/ev-charging-station-earnings/) explains the method.

## Choose the location first

A good charger in the wrong place stays idle. Before you look at chargers, look at the site:

- **Vehicles that pass or park there.** Cars, cabs, e-scooters, e-rickshaws, buses or trucks. Cab, delivery and e-rickshaw fleets that run past every day can become regular customers early on.
- **How long they stay.** The length of the stop decides the charger (see the table below).
- **Access and visibility.** Easy in and out from the road, visible from it, lit at night, with a safe place to wait. Food, toilets or a shop nearby help on highways.
- **Power nearby.** The distance to the nearest DISCOM transformer or line affects what the connection costs and how long it takes.
- **Other chargers.** Check charging apps for stations close by, what they offer and what they charge.
- **Mobile signal.** Chargers talk to their software over the internet, so check the signal where they will stand.
- **Space.** A bay for each charging gun, room to drive in and out, and space for panels or, on HT sites, a transformer.

| Site | How long vehicles stay | What usually fits |
|---|---|---|
| Highway stop, fuel station or dhaba | A short break or a meal | DC fast chargers, 60 kW and up |
| City hub on cab routes | Under an hour | DC fast chargers, 30–120 kW |
| Mall, cinema or restaurant | One to three hours | AC chargers, plus a DC charger for quick top-ups |
| Hotel, office, hospital or housing society | Several hours or overnight | AC chargers, 7.4 or 11 kW |
| E-scooter and e-rickshaw hub | Between shifts or overnight | 2W and 3W chargers, socket points |
| Bus or truck depot | Overnight and between trips | High-power DC chargers |

## AC or DC, and how many chargers

**AC chargers** are simpler and need far less power. The charger feeds the car's on-board charger, which sets the speed: 3.3 kW on a 16 A single-phase supply, 7.4 kW on 32 A single phase, and 11 or 22 kW on three phase. Many cars in India accept 7.2 kW on AC and some accept 11 kW, so a 22 kW AC charger adds no speed for those cars. Cars use the Type 2 connector.

**DC fast chargers** convert the power inside the charger and feed the battery directly. Most new cars use the CCS2 connector, though older Bharat DC-001 and GB/T chargers still exist. Chargers of 30–60 kW run on a three-phase LT connection, while 120–240 kW units need a high LT load or an HT connection with a transformer. Dual-gun chargers share their power when two vehicles charge at once. See [DC fast chargers](/chargers/dc-fast-chargers/).

**Light EVs** use their own connectors: IS 17017-2-6 "Type 6" for AC and IS 17017-2-7 "Type 7" (LECCS) for DC. If e-scooters and e-rickshaws are your market, see [2W and 3W chargers](/chargers/2w-3w-chargers/).

How many? For a fleet or depot, work backwards: vehicles × kilometres a day ÷ kilometres per unit gives the energy you must deliver each day, and dividing that by the hours the vehicles stand idle gives the charging power you need. For a public station, start with the chargers your first year's demand supports and build for the next step: lay conduits and size the trench, panel and canopy for the chargers you expect to add, so you don't dig up the site twice. Ask for the load you need now if your DISCOM lets you raise it later, because a higher sanctioned load can mean a higher fixed charge every month.

Two more checks before you buy:

- **Software support.** Choose chargers that support OCPP, the open protocol chargers use to talk to charging software, so you can change software later without changing chargers.
- **Service in your state.** A charger that is out of order loses the drivers you worked to win, so ask each brand how it handles repairs and spares where your station is.

The [charger finder](/tools/charger-finder/) suggests a charger type and power for your vehicles and site, and our advice on brands is free.

## The electricity connection

Your station cannot open until its connection is live, so start on it as soon as the site is fixed.

- **A separate meter.** The Ministry of Power's 2024 guidelines call for a separate metering arrangement for each charging station, and let DISCOMs provide a sub-meter behind an existing HT connection, which helps hotels, malls and factories that already have one ([Mercom, Sep 2024](https://www.mercomindia.com/ministry-of-power-releases-ev-charging-infrastructure-guidelines)).
- **The EV tariff.** The same guidelines suggest a single-part tariff for public charging, capped at the average cost of supply until 31 March 2028, 30% cheaper in solar hours and 30% dearer outside them ([Lok Sabha answer, Aug 2025](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/2994845/1/AU3099_jxi0H3.pdf)). Your state's electricity regulatory commission decides how this applies, in its tariff order.
- **The load.** Add up the input power of every charger you plan, plus lights and anything else on the same supply. DC chargers draw a little more from the grid than they deliver, because some energy is lost in conversion.
- **LT or HT.** The guidelines let charging stations take an LT connection for loads up to 150 kW ([Lok Sabha answer, Dec 2024](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/2985666/1/AS254_WdMGkS.pdf)), but what your DISCOM offers depends on your state's rules. Above the LT limit you need an HT connection at 11 kV or more, with your own transformer and HT panel, which adds cost, space and time.
- **Timelines.** The guidelines also set time limits, from 3 to 90 days, for DISCOMs to release connections to charging stations ([Power Line, Sep 2024](https://powerline.net.in/2024/09/27/ministry-of-power-releases-guidelines-for-installation-and-operation-of-electric-vehicle-charging-infrastructure-2024/)). In Delhi, the EV Policy 2026 plans a single-window facility to help charging operators get approvals and electricity connections faster ([Daily Pioneer, Jul 2026](https://www.dailypioneer.com/news/slug-lite/delhi-notifies-ev-policy-2026-subsidies-within-60-days?year=2026)).

Questions to ask your DISCOM:

1. Which tariff category applies to a public EV charging station here, and does it have time-of-day rates or a fixed charge per kW?
2. Can the station have its own connection and meter at this address, or a sub-meter behind our existing HT connection?
3. Up to what load can we take LT supply, and when do we need HT?
4. Is there spare capacity on the nearest transformer and line, or will they need upgrading, and who pays for that work?
5. Which documents do you need: proof of ownership or lease, ID, NOCs, load details, drawings?
6. How long will the connection take, and which inspections are needed before the chargers are switched on?

We tell you the load to apply for, whether LT or HT suits your plan, and which documents to keep ready, so your application has the right details from the start.

## Land: own, lease or revenue share

- **Your own land** means no rent and full control, but money tied up in the site. Check with your local authority that the land can be used for a commercial charging station.
- **A lease** means a fixed rent whatever the usage, which hurts in the quiet first months. Agree a term long enough to recover your investment, the right to lay cables, build a canopy and put up signs, access at the hours you plan to open, and what happens to the equipment when the lease ends.
- **A revenue share** pays the landowner part of what drivers pay, instead of rent or alongside a smaller one. It costs less in quiet months and more in busy ones, and it suits malls, hotels and fuel stations, whose owners may also gain from the extra visitors.
- **Government and public land.** The 2024 guidelines provide for land to be made available for public charging stations, at reduced rates to government and public bodies and through a revenue-sharing model to any entity ([Lok Sabha answer, Dec 2024](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/2985666/1/AS254_WdMGkS.pdf)).

Whichever you choose, put it in writing, including who keeps the charging bays free of parked petrol and diesel cars. The earnings calculator takes either rent or a revenue share, so you can compare the two.

## Franchise, partnership or going independent

Many charging networks offer franchise or partnership models. The split of roles differs from one network to the next: in some, you bring the land and the investment and the network brings its brand, app and operations; in others, the network invests and pays you for the use of your site.

- **With a network,** drivers find you in an app they already use, and you get the network's support. In return you share revenue and may have to follow its choice of chargers, software and prices.
- **On your own,** you choose the chargers, set the price, pick the software and keep all the revenue. You also bring in the drivers yourself, through maps, apps, signs and tie-ups with fleets, and you look after maintenance and driver calls.

Before you sign with any network, get clear answers to these questions:

- Who owns the chargers, and who pays for repairs?
- Who sets the charging price?
- What share of revenue does each side keep, and what fees do you pay?
- How long are you tied in, and how can you exit?
- Who owns the customer data?
- Is any income promised, and on what conditions?

## Software: app platforms or your own CMS

Every public charger needs software behind it: to start and stop sessions, take payment, issue invoices, show the station on a map and alert you when a charger goes offline. There are two routes, and you can combine them.

- **An app platform or aggregator.** You list your chargers on an app drivers already use, and the platform handles payment and driver support for a commission or fee. It is the quickest start, but you have less say over pricing, branding and the customer relationship.
- **Your own charge management system (CMS).** Your brand on the app, the QR codes and the receipts, your prices, and payments into your own account. In return, you handle driver support yourself.

Proline makes its own CMS. [Proline CMS](/cms/) runs chargers of different brands from one console, in your brand and on your own server, and drivers scan a QR code and pay by UPI. Whichever route you choose, buy chargers that support OCPP, so your software decision is never locked to the hardware.

## Subsidies: what PM E-DRIVE does and does not do

The central PM E-DRIVE scheme supports public charging stations, but it is not an automatic grant for every station. Proposals go through government nodal agencies, and the support is approval-based, not automatic ([ChargeZone](https://www.chargezone.co.in/franchise)). The highest support, up to 100%, is for stations at government premises that are open free to the public; busy public places such as transport hubs, malls and highway stops can get partial support ([AIR News, Sep 2025](https://www.newsonair.gov.in/centre-issues-guidelines-for-rollout-of-72300-ev-charging-stations-under-pm-e-drive/)).

Build your plan so it works without a subsidy, and treat any support as a bonus if your site qualifies. Check your state's EV policy as well.
