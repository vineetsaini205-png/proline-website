## Tubular lead-acid or lithium (LFP)

Most home inverters in India run on tubular lead-acid batteries. Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) batteries do the same job, and they suit daily solar use and long power cuts better.

| | Tubular lead-acid | Lithium (LFP) |
|---|---|---|
| Usable capacity | About 50% for a long life; up to 80% now and then | 80–90%, every day |
| Life | About 1,000–1,500 cycles on makers' datasheets | Often 3,000–6,000 cycles |
| Maintenance | Distilled water top-ups as often as the maker says; clean terminals | None beyond keeping it clean and dry |
| Charging | Slow; takes many hours to fill completely | Fast; accepts a high charging current |
| Weight and space | Heavy: around 50–60 kg for a 12 V, 150 Ah battery | A fraction of the weight; often wall-mounted |
| Ventilation | Needed: gives off hydrogen while charging | Sealed; no gas in normal use |
| Cost to buy | Lower | Higher |
| Suits | Short, occasional power cuts | Daily solar use, long or frequent cuts |

Cycle figures compare only at the same depth of discharge and temperature, so read them with the conditions the maker states. Sealed maintenance-free (SMF or VRLA) batteries, common in computer UPS units, need no top-ups but last fewer cycles, so they suit short backup.

## How to size the battery: a worked example

Start from the load you want in a power cut, not from the battery.

1. **List the load.** Three ceiling fans at 75 W (225 W), five LED lights at 15 W (75 W), an LED TV (80 W) and a Wi-Fi router (20 W): 400 W in all.
2. **Multiply by the backup hours.** 400 W for 4 hours is 1,600 Wh.
3. **Allow for inverter losses.** A home inverter loses roughly 15%, so the battery must supply about 1,600 ÷ 0.85 ≈ 1,900 Wh.
4. **Divide by the depth of discharge you plan.**
   - Tubular, used to 50%: 1,900 ÷ 0.5 = 3,800 Wh. On a 24 V inverter that is 3,800 ÷ 24 ≈ 160 Ah, so two 12 V tubular batteries of about 160 Ah.
   - Lithium, used to 90%: 1,900 ÷ 0.9 ≈ 2,100 Wh, which a 25.6 V, 100 Ah LFP battery (2.56 kWh) covers with room to spare.

The same backup needs about 3.8 kWh of tubular batteries but only about 2.1 kWh of lithium, because lithium gives more of its capacity.

Two more checks. The inverter must carry the load: its VA rating should be well above your load in watts, because fans and pumps draw extra current as they start. And leave out heavy loads such as air conditioners, geysers, irons and water pumps, unless you size the whole system for them.

On lead-acid labels, check whether the Ah is given at the C10 or C20 rate, that is, over 10 or 20 hours. The same battery shows more Ah at C20, and a 4-hour backup draws it faster than either, so it delivers less than the label says.

## Depth of discharge and cycle life

Depth of discharge (DoD) is how much of the battery's capacity you use before you charge it again. The deeper the discharge, the fewer cycles the battery lasts, and lead-acid feels this most, which is why the example above uses tubular batteries only to half their capacity.

A daily solar cycle adds up to about 365 cycles a year. A battery made for 1,000 to 1,500 cycles can be worn out in three or four years of solar use, while an LFP battery made for 3,000 cycles or more can last much longer. Heat matters too: every battery ages faster when it is hot, so keep it out of the sun and away from the kitchen.

## Batteries for home solar: on-grid, hybrid or off-grid

- **On-grid, no battery.** Panels power your home and send any surplus to the grid where net metering is in place. It is the simplest way to cut your bill, but it switches off in a power cut by design, so it never feeds a line that workers are repairing.
- **Hybrid.** A hybrid inverter runs the panels, battery and grid together. It powers your home from the sun, charges the battery, can export the surplus, and switches to the battery when the power goes.
- **Off-grid.** Panels, a battery and an off-grid inverter or solar PCU, with no export. It suits places where the grid is weak or absent, and the battery must carry the whole night's load.

Size the panels to refill the battery as well as run your daytime load. In India, 1 kWp of panels makes about 4 to 5.5 units (kWh) on a clear sunny day ([MNRE rooftop solar FAQ](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2022/apr/doc202242850101.pdf)), and less on cloudy days and in winter. Refilling 2 kWh a day takes roughly half a kWp of panels on top of what your daytime load uses.

With lithium, make sure the inverter can charge it properly. Many hybrid inverters talk to the battery's BMS over a data cable, so they charge exactly as the battery asks; check that your battery is on the inverter maker's compatibility list.

Exporting to the grid needs net metering, or your state's equivalent, from your DISCOM. You apply to the DISCOM, and we tell you what the application needs. Our [solar installation](/services/solar-installation/) page explains how a rooftop system is planned.

## Where the battery goes

- A dry, ventilated spot, away from flames, gas cylinders and the kitchen stove. Lead-acid batteries need fresh air while they charge.
- On a stand or trolley, off a damp floor, with room to reach the caps for top-ups.
- Out of direct sun, and out of reach of children.
- Close to the inverter, with short cables of the right size and a fuse or DC breaker near the battery.
