## What a BESS is

A battery energy storage system is more than batteries in a box. A complete system has five parts:

- **Battery modules,** usually lithium iron phosphate (LFP), which is more stable in heat and lasts more cycles than NMC.
- **A battery management system (BMS)** that watches every cell's voltage and temperature, balances the cells and disconnects the battery on a fault.
- **A power conversion system (PCS):** the inverter that charges the battery from AC and turns its DC back into AC. Its kW rating sets how fast energy goes in and out.
- **An energy management system (EMS):** the controller that decides when to charge and when to discharge, from your site's load, the solar output, the tariff hours and the limits you set.
- **The enclosure:** a cabinet or container with its own cooling, fire detection and suppression, and protection.

There are two common layouts. A **stand-alone system** connects to your LT panel, so it can support every charger on the site and store solar. A **charger with built-in storage** carries its own battery, so it can deliver more power than its grid supply for a while; it suits a single fast charger on a weak supply.

## When a BESS pays, and when it doesn't

1. **Grid-limited sites.** If your sanctioned load, or the transformer nearby, can't supply the DC chargers you want, a battery fills the gap during sessions and refills between them. It can be an alternative to a load increase or an HT connection with its own transformer, both of which take time and money.
2. **Peak and fixed charges.** For public charging stations, the Ministry of Power's 2024 guidelines suggest a tariff 30% cheaper in solar hours (0.7 times) and 30% dearer outside them (1.3 times) ([Lok Sabha answer, Aug 2025](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/2994845/1/AU3099_jxi0H3.pdf)); your state commission sets the actual rates and hours. A unit stored at midday and used in the evening costs much less, even after the 10–15% lost in the battery's round trip. Some states also add a fixed charge for every kW of load ([BEE state-wise EV tariffs](https://beeindia.gov.in/sites/default/files/annexure-A.pdf)), and a battery that covers your peaks can let you keep a lower sanctioned load.
3. **Midday solar for evening sessions.** Panels make the most power around noon, while many public stations are busiest in the evening. A battery moves the surplus to the evening, when grid power costs more. See [solar EV charging](/solar-ev-charging/).
4. **Backup.** Where power cuts are frequent, a system built for backup keeps chargers, lights and the internet router running, so sessions don't stop halfway.

It usually does not pay when:

- Your connection already covers your chargers, and your tariff has little gap between hours.
- The station is new and quiet. Usage at a new station typically builds over 6–12 months ([Bolt.Earth](https://bolt.earth/franchise)), and a battery adds cost before the sessions arrive. You can leave space and cable routes for one and add it later.
- You need high power for many hours every day. The battery would have to be very large, and a bigger grid connection is the better answer.

## Sizing basics: kW and kWh

A battery has two sizes, and you need both:

- **Power (kW):** how fast it can deliver energy. It must cover the gap between what your chargers draw and what the grid gives.
- **Energy (kWh):** how long it can keep that up. It must last through your busiest stretch.

A worked example: a highway site has 40 kW of its LT connection free for charging and wants to offer one 60 kW DC charger.

1. **The power gap.** A 60 kW DC charger draws about 64 kW from its supply, because about 6% is lost in conversion. The battery must supply 64 − 40 = 24 kW.
2. **The busiest stretch.** In the evening, three cars charge back to back for 30 minutes each: 1.5 hours. To stay on the safe side, assume full power throughout.
3. **Energy from the battery.** 24 kW × 1.5 hours = 36 kWh.
4. **Usable share and ageing.** With 90% of the capacity usable, and planning for the battery to hold 80% of its first capacity late in its life: 36 ÷ 0.9 ÷ 0.8 = 50 kWh.
5. **Power check.** A battery that discharges at 0.5C delivers, in kW, half its kWh: 50 kWh × 0.5 = 25 kW, just enough for the 24 kW gap. A 1C battery would give 50 kW.
6. **Refill.** When the charger is idle, the battery refills from the spare 40 kW or from solar, and the 36 kWh it used comes back in about an hour.

The result is a battery of about 50 kWh with an inverter of at least 25 kW. If a fourth car arrives straight after the third, it charges at what the grid can give once the battery runs low, so the EMS or the charging software must cap the charger's power and keep the site within its sanctioned load.

For a real site, the best data is your chargers' session log (the energy and start time of each session), a year of electricity bills, and your solar output if you have panels.

## Choosing a system

- **Chemistry.** LFP is the usual choice for storage at charging sites, for its stability in heat and its cycle life.
- **Size and form.** Cabinets from tens of kWh to a few hundred kWh suit most stations; containers of a MWh or more suit depots and large hubs.
- **Warranty.** Makers usually state it in years and in total energy delivered (MWh), with the capacity the battery should still hold at the end. It comes from the BESS maker, so check who services the system near your site.
- **Cooling.** Air or liquid cooling, and an operating temperature range that suits your summers.
- **EMS modes.** Check it does what you need: peak shaving, storing solar, time-of-day shifting, backup, and capping charger power.
- **Test reports.** Ask for the battery's safety tests, such as IEC 62619, and a fire propagation test, such as UL 9540A, for the system.

## Fire safety, location and ventilation

LFP is less prone to thermal runaway than NMC, but a large battery still needs a safe place and the right protection:

- **Location.** Outdoors on a raised plinth where possible, above flood level, away from building exits and parked cars, with the clear space the maker's manual asks for. At a fuel station, ask the oil company where storage may stand.
- **Protection from vehicles.** Bollards or a kerb, so no car can hit the cabinet. See [civil work](/services/ev-charging-civil-work/).
- **Shade and air.** Heat shortens battery life. Keep cabinets in shade where you can, with clear space around their coolers. Indoors, use a ventilated room, separated from the rest of the building as the maker specifies.
- **Detection and suppression.** Smoke, heat or gas detection inside the enclosure, automatic suppression, an emergency stop, extinguishers suitable for electrical fires, and clear access for the fire brigade.
- **Electrical protection.** AC and DC protection, surge protection and earthing to IS 3043, installed to the maker's manual and the CEA safety regulations.

Ask your DISCOM and your state's electrical inspector which approvals a battery system at your site needs; we tell you what to prepare.
