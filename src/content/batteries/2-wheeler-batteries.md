## What is inside an electric scooter battery

A scooter battery pack has three parts, and each one matters when you choose a replacement:

- **Cells.** Dozens of lithium-ion cells, often cylindrical, joined in series to reach the pack's voltage and in parallel to reach its capacity.
- **The battery management system (BMS).** The electronics that watch every cell and switch the pack off before anything goes wrong.
- **The case.** A metal or plastic housing that keeps out water and dust, holds the cells in place and carries the connector to your scooter.

Two packs with the same label can differ a lot in all three.

## NMC or LFP: which chemistry

Most electric scooters in India use one of two lithium-ion chemistries.

| | NMC (nickel manganese cobalt) | LFP (lithium iron phosphate) |
|---|---|---|
| Cell voltage | 3.6–3.7 V, 4.2 V when full | 3.2 V, 3.65 V when full |
| Weight for the same energy | Lighter and smaller | Heavier and larger |
| Typical cycle life | About 1,000–2,000 cycles | About 2,000–4,000 cycles |
| In heat | Ages faster; needs a good BMS and cooling | More stable; less prone to thermal runaway |
| Suits | Range from a small, light pack | Daily heavy use, fleets, hot cities |

Cycle life is counted until the pack holds about 80% of its first capacity. The figures are typical; the exact pack's datasheet is what counts.

NMC gives more range for its size and weight. LFP needs a little more space for the same range, but it lasts more cycles and stays calmer in heat, which counts in cities where summer temperatures pass 45 °C.

## Voltage and capacity: reading the label

The voltage, set by the number of cells in series, must match your scooter's controller and motor. Most electric scooters in India run on 48, 60 or 72 V, with the higher voltages on faster, more powerful models.

Capacity is given in ampere-hours (Ah). Multiply it by the voltage to get the energy in watt-hours, which is what sets your range:

- 48 V × 30 Ah = 1,440 Wh, about 1.4 kWh
- 60 V × 40 Ah = 2,400 Wh, about 2.4 kWh
- 72 V × 40 Ah = 2,880 Wh, about 2.9 kWh

So compare packs in kWh, not Ah. A 72 V, 30 Ah pack (2.2 kWh) holds more energy than a 48 V, 40 Ah pack (1.9 kWh), even though its Ah is lower.

For a rough range, divide the usable watt-hours by the watt-hours your scooter uses per kilometre: often 25 to 40 Wh per km in city riding, more at high speed, with a pillion or on hills. At 30 Wh per km, a 2.4 kWh pack gives roughly 80 km. The usable energy is a little below the total, because the BMS keeps a reserve.

## What the BMS does

The BMS is the pack's safety system. A good one:

- Watches the voltage of each cell group, the pack's temperature and the current.
- Stops charging when any cell is full, and stops discharge before any cell is empty.
- Cuts the pack off on a short circuit, too much current or too much heat.
- Balances the cells, so they stay at the same state of charge.
- On many scooters, talks to the controller and dashboard: it shows the charge left and limits power when the pack is hot or nearly empty.

That last point matters for replacements: a pack whose BMS doesn't talk to your scooter the way it expects may not run it, or may leave you without a charge display.

## Matching the charger

A charger is built for one chemistry and one pack voltage. It charges at a set current, then holds a final voltage until the pack is full, and that final voltage depends on the cells:

- A 48 V NMC pack of 13 cells in series finishes at about 54.6 V (4.2 V per cell).
- A 51.2 V LFP pack of 16 cells in series finishes at about 58.4 V (3.65 V per cell).

A charger set for the wrong chemistry or number of cells either leaves the pack part-charged or pushes it past its limit, and then only the BMS stands between the pack and damage. So:

- **Use the charger made for the pack,** or one its maker names as suitable.
- **Check the charging current.** A higher current charges faster but warms the pack more.
- **Give it a proper socket.** Most scooters charge from a portable charger, so use a socket on a circuit with an MCB, an RCD and a sound earth, not a thin extension cord.
- **Public charging.** Newer scooters may also use India's light-EV connectors: IS 17017-2-6 "Type 6" for DC charging, and IS 17017-2-7 "Type 7" (LECCS), which takes both AC and DC charging through one inlet. See [2W and 3W chargers](/chargers/2w-3w-chargers/).

## What shortens a pack's life

- **Heat.** The biggest one in India. Park in the shade, and let a hot pack cool before you charge it.
- **Deep discharge.** Running to empty every day wears a pack faster than charging at 20 to 30%.
- **Sitting full.** Leaving a pack at 100% for days, especially in the heat, ages it faster. Where you can, finish charging close to when you ride.
- **Long storage.** For a long break, leave the pack about half charged in a cool, dry place, and check it every month or so.

## AIS-156: India's battery safety rules for e-scooters

After electric scooter fires in 2022, the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways amended AIS-156, the safety standard for electric two-wheelers, three-wheelers and quadricycles (the L category). Amendment 3 added requirements for the battery cells, the BMS, the on-board charger, pack design and thermal propagation, applied in two phases from 1 December 2022 and 31 March 2023 ([Autocar Pro, Sep 2022](https://autocarpro.in/news-national/government-extends-ev-battery-safety-norms-deadline-102906)). Among the added tests, a fully charged pack must go through an IPX7 water-immersion test without fire or explosion, and a single cell going into thermal runaway must not set off a fire or explosion, with an audio-visual warning for early detection ([EVreporter, ARAI guide](https://evreporter.com/battery-safety-standards-in-india-by-arai/)).

What it means when you buy a pack:

- Ask for the AIS-156 test report for that exact pack model, from a testing agency such as ARAI or ICAT.
- Be careful with packs that come without a test report, BMS details or a written warranty.
- Charge it only with the matching charger.

## Replace, upgrade or swap

- **Like for like.** The scooter maker's own pack keeps the scooter working as designed. Check the warranty first: a pack that lost capacity early may still be covered.
- **A compatible pack.** For an older scooter, or one whose maker no longer supplies packs, a pack from another maker can work if the voltage, size, connector, BMS current limit and controller communication all match.
- **More range.** More Ah at the same voltage gives more range if it fits and the BMS can supply the current your controller draws. A higher voltage needs a matching controller and charger, and may affect your warranty.
- **Swap instead of own.** On a battery-swapping network you exchange an empty pack for a charged one at a station in a few minutes, and the network owns the batteries. It suits delivery and taxi riders who ride all day, but the scooter must be built for that network's packs, and you depend on where its stations are and what it charges.

Old packs need care too: don't open one or throw it in the bin, because damaged lithium cells can catch fire. Under the Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022, which cover EV batteries, producers must collect waste batteries and send them for recycling or refurbishment ([Autocar Pro, Aug 2022](https://autocarpro.in/news-national/centre-notifies-new-battery-waste-management-norms-92626)), so ask the seller how your old pack will be taken back.
