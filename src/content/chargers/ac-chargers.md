## What a 7.4 kW charger does

An AC charger, often called a wall box, does not charge the battery by itself. It supplies AC power safely, and the car's onboard charger turns it into DC for the battery. So the onboard charger's rating sets how fast the car charges on AC. Check it in your car's manual or spec sheet before you choose a charger.

- **A car that accepts 7.2 kW** takes its full AC speed from a 7.4 kW wall box. Many cars sold in India are in this group.
- **A car with a 3.3 kW onboard charger** charges at 3.3 kW, whatever wall box you fit.
- **A car that accepts 11 kW** needs a three-phase 11 kW charger to charge faster than 7.4 kW.

Cars sold in India use the Type 2 connector for AC charging, so a Type 2 charger fits them all. The difference between models is power, features and build.

| Charger | Supply | Current | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.3 kW socket point or portable charger | Single phase | 16 A | Overnight top-ups, cars with a 3.3 kW onboard charger |
| 7.4 kW wall box | Single phase | 32 A | Homes, villas and office parking |
| 11 kW wall box or pedestal | Three phase | 16 A per phase | Cars that accept 11 kW, offices and societies |
| 22 kW wall box or pedestal | Three phase | 32 A per phase | Vehicles that accept 22 kW, twin-socket units for two cars |

A quick way to estimate charging time: divide the energy you want to add, in kWh, by the lower of the charger's power and the car's onboard charger, then add 10 to 15% for losses. At 7.2 kW, adding 30 kWh takes about four and a half to five hours, so an overnight charge fills most cars.

A word on 22 kW. It needs a three-phase supply and heavier cable, yet a car that accepts 7.2 kW still charges at 7.2 kW on it, and one that accepts 11 kW charges at 11 kW. A 22 kW charger pays off only for vehicles that accept 22 kW, or as a twin-socket unit that shares its power between two cars.

## Portable charger, wall box or pedestal

- **Portable charger:** plugs into a 16 A socket and charges at up to about 3.3 kW. It is a useful backup and works where you park in different places. The socket should be on its own circuit with an MCB, an RCD and a sound earth, because the charger draws a heavy current for hours.
- **Wall box:** fixed on a wall next to your parking, from 3.3 to 22 kW. This is the usual choice at homes and offices. Choose a tethered cable, fixed to the charger, for convenience, or a Type 2 socket where drivers bring their own cable.
- **Pedestal:** stands on its own foundation in open parking, or where no wall is close to the car. Pedestals often carry two sockets for two bays, and need a small foundation. See [civil work](/services/ev-charging-civil-work/).

## Smart features worth comparing

- **App and connectivity:** Wi-Fi or Bluetooth for a home app; 4G or Ethernet where Wi-Fi does not reach, such as a basement.
- **OCPP 1.6J:** the open protocol that connects a charger to a charge management system (CMS). You need it to bill users in a society, office or public car park, and it leaves you free to change software later. [Proline CMS](/cms/) is our own software for running OCPP chargers.
- **RFID cards or app login:** so only your family, residents or staff can start a charge.
- **Scheduling:** start charging at the hours your tariff is lowest, where your tariff changes with the time of day.
- **Load balancing:** a sensor on your incoming supply tells the charger how much the rest of the house is drawing. The charger slows down when the AC and geyser are on, so the total stays within your sanctioned load.
- **Built-in energy meter and display:** shows the energy used in each session, which helps when a charger is shared or billed.
- **DC leakage detection:** some chargers have 6 mA DC fault detection built in, which lets the DB box use a Type A RCD; others need a Type B RCD. Ask before you buy, because it changes the installation.
- **IP and IK ratings:** IP54 or higher keeps out dust and rain at an outdoor spot; a higher IK rating matters where a car or trolley can knock the charger.

## Comparing brands: features, warranty and service

We don't make chargers. We are tied up with many charger brands, so our advice starts from your car and your site, not from one catalogue. When we suggest options, usually two or three, we compare them on the features above and on these points:

- **Test reports for the exact model.** Ask which lab tested it, such as ARAI or ICAT, and against which standard, such as IS 17017, India's standard for EV charging.
- **Warranty.** How long it lasts, whether it covers parts and labour, and whether repairs happen at your site or the unit goes back to the maker.
- **Who services it in your city.** A long warranty helps only if someone can reach you to honour it.
- **The app's future.** Whether the app needs the maker's cloud account, whether the charger still works without internet, and whether firmware updates arrive over the air.
- **The installation manual.** The cable size, breaker, RCD type and earthing the maker asks for. We follow it on every job.

## What the installation needs

A 7.4 kW charger draws 32 A for hours at a time, so the work behind it matters as much as the charger.

- **Its own circuit** from your meter or main DB, with cable sized for 32 A of continuous current and the length of the run.
- **A DB box** with an MCB, the RCD type the maker asks for, and surge protection. See [EV charger DB boxes](/services/ev-charger-db-box/).
- **A sound earth,** often a new, dedicated earth pit, tested for resistance.
- **Enough sanctioned load.** A 7.4 kW charger adds up to 7.4 kW to your home's demand. If that takes you over your limit, you need a load increase, or a charger with load balancing that keeps you under it. 11 and 22 kW chargers need a three-phase supply.
- **Permission where needed,** such as a society NOC for a charger in common parking.
