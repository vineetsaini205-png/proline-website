## What a 60 kW DC charger does

An [AC wall box](/chargers/ac-chargers/) leaves the conversion to the car's onboard charger, which is why most cars charge at 11 kW or less on AC. A DC charger does the conversion itself: it turns three-phase AC into DC in its power modules and feeds the battery directly. That is why it charges many times faster, and why it needs a far bigger supply.

The car still decides how fast it charges. Its battery management system (BMS) tells the charger, moment by moment, how much current it will accept. In real-world tests in India in 2026, mainstream cars peaked at about 65 to 95 kW on DC, and premium models at 125 kW or more ([Autocar India, Sep 2026](https://www.autocarindia.com/auto-features/real-world-charging-times-of-all-evs-tested-in-2026-441013)). So a 60 kW gun gives many cars most of the speed they can take, and a bigger charger makes sense where cars that take more power stop, or where one charger serves two cars at once.

| Charger | Guns | Supply it usually needs | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 kW | One, sometimes two | Three-phase LT, about 45–50 A per phase | Dealerships, offices, sites with limited load |
| 60 kW | One or two | Three-phase LT, about 90–100 A per phase | City stations, fleets, dealerships |
| 120 kW | Usually two | LT if the site's total load is within the limit, otherwise HT | Busy city hubs, highways, cab fleets |
| 180–240 kW | Two or more | Usually HT with its own transformer | Highways, bus and truck depots, large hubs |

## Why cars rarely take full power for the whole session

A car takes its highest power when the battery is low and at a comfortable temperature. As the battery fills, the BMS lowers the current to protect the cells, gently at first and steeply near full. Each model has its own charging curve: in the same tests, one car held its peak only to about half charge, while another kept a steady rate from 20 to 80 per cent. A very hot or very cold battery also takes less.

This shapes the charger you buy:

- **Many drivers unplug at about 80 per cent,** because the last part of a charge is slow.
- **Dual-gun chargers use the slack.** When one car slows near full, a charger that shares power dynamically moves the spare power to the other gun.
- **A station sells energy, not kW.** It earns on the kWh it delivers each day, which depends on how many cars stop and how much they take, more than on the peak rating.

## Single gun, dual gun and power sharing

A DC charger is built from power modules, usually of 20 to 40 kW each: a 60 kW charger might hold two 30 kW modules, and a 120 kW charger four. How it uses them decides how it behaves with two cars:

- **Single gun:** all the power goes to one car. Simple, and right where one car at a time is the norm.
- **Dual gun, equal sharing:** one car can take the full output; with two cars, each gets up to half.
- **Dual gun, dynamic sharing:** modules move to whichever car can use them, so when one car slows down, the other speeds up. Ask which kind you are buying, and whether both guns can charge at once.
- **Power cabinet with dispensers:** at large sites, one cabinet feeds several dispensers at the bays and shares its modules among them, and you add dispensers as demand grows. This is common at depots; see [bus and truck chargers](/chargers/bus-truck-chargers/).

## Guns, cables and cooling

- **CCS2** is the DC connector on most new cars sold in India: a Type 2 inlet with two large DC pins below it, so one inlet takes both AC and DC.
- **Bharat DC-001** chargers use the GB/T connector and give up to 15 kW to vehicles with batteries under 100 V ([EVreporter](https://evreporter.com/future-of-bharat-charging-standard-dc-001/)). They served the first low-voltage electric cars, and few vehicles sold today use them.
- **Current per gun** matters as much as the cabinet's kW. Power is voltage times current, so a gun built for 200 A gives a car with a 400 V battery about 80 kW at most, whatever the cabinet's rating.
- **Cable cooling.** Ordinary CCS2 cables are made for a set current, commonly 200 or 250 A. The highest-power guns use liquid-cooled cables, which carry more current without becoming too heavy to handle.
- **Output voltage.** Most cars sold in India use batteries in the 400 V class. A few premium models use 800 V systems, which reach full speed only on chargers whose output goes up to about 1,000 V.
- **Cabinet cooling.** Most DC chargers are cooled by fans that pull air through filters, which clog fast at dusty sites; some high-power units use liquid-cooled modules in a sealed cabinet. Many also cut their output in very hot air, so ask at what temperature this starts, and give the cabinet shade.

Scooters and e-rickshaws use their own connectors; see [2W and 3W chargers](/chargers/2w-3w-chargers/).

## Features worth comparing

- **OCPP 1.6J:** the open protocol that connects a charger to a charge management system (CMS) over 4G or Ethernet, to start sessions, take payment and report faults. It leaves you free to change software later. [Proline CMS](/cms/) is our own software for OCPP chargers of any make.
- **How drivers start and pay:** a touch screen, RFID cards, and a QR code that opens a UPI payment page on the driver's phone.
- **DC energy meter:** drivers are billed on its reading, so ask for its accuracy class.
- **Remote diagnostics and over-the-air firmware updates,** so faults can be checked, and some fixed, without a site visit.
- **IP and IK ratings:** IP54 or higher outdoors, and a higher IK rating where vehicles pass close by.

We don't make chargers. We are tied up with many charger brands, so we compare DC chargers for your site rather than sell from one catalogue, on the features above and on these points:

- **Test reports for the exact model:** which lab tested it, such as ARAI or ICAT, and against which standard, such as IS 17017, India's standard for EV charging.
- **Warranty:** how long it lasts, whether it covers parts, labour and power modules, and whether repairs happen on site.
- **Spares:** whether power modules, guns, cables, fans and filters are stocked in India, and how quickly they reach your state.
- **Local service:** a DC charger that is down loses its drivers, so who services it in your state matters more than a long warranty on paper.
- **Software:** whether the model has already run on the CMS you plan to use.
- **The installation manual:** the cable size, breaker, protection and earthing the maker asks for. We follow it on every job.

## What the installation needs

A 60 kW charger draws roughly 90–100 A per phase while a car takes full power, so the work behind it matters as much as the charger.

- **Enough supply.** The central guidelines let charging stations take an LT connection for loads up to 150 kW ([Lok Sabha answer, December 2024](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/2985666/1/AS254_WdMGkS.pdf)), so one or two 60 kW chargers can often run on LT. Above that, or where your state's rules differ, you need an HT connection with a transformer.
- **An LT panel** with a breaker for each charger, the earth-fault protection the maker asks for, surge protection and metering.
- **Armoured cable** sized for the input current and the length of the run, in a trench or pipe.
- **A plinth, bollards and dedicated earth pits,** with the earth tested for resistance.
- **Commissioning:** electrical tests, the OCPP connection to your CMS and a test charge.
