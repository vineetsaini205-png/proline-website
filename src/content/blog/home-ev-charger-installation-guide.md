To install an EV charger at home, check what your car and charger need, make sure your electricity connection has load to spare, and have a qualified electrician run a dedicated circuit with its own DB box, RCD and tested earth to a charger mounted beside your parking spot. In a housing society, you also need the society's permission before work starts.

You can do the first three steps yourself with your car's manual and your electricity bill, and the [installation checklist](/tools/installation-checklist/) lists what your own home needs.

## Before you start: what to check

| What to check | Where to find it | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Your car's maximum AC charging power | Owner's manual or spec sheet | Sets the charger size worth buying |
| What came with the car | The car's kit | You may not need to buy a charger |
| Sanctioned load and phase | Your electricity bill | Shows whether the charger fits without a load increase |
| The charger's current and RCD type | The charger's installation manual | Sets the cable, MCB and RCD |
| Distance from meter or main DB to parking | Measure it | Sets the cable size and route |
| Society rules | Society office or RWA | Permission is usually needed first |

## Step 1: Check what your car can take

Every electric car has an on-board charger that limits how much AC power it accepts. Many cars sold in India take 7.2 kW, some take 11 kW, and a few small cars take only 3.3 kW. A wall box bigger than your car can use needs more supply and does not charge any faster.

Charging time is roughly the battery size divided by the power your car takes, plus about 10% for losses: a 30 kWh battery takes about four and a half hours from empty at 7.2 kW. If you charge overnight, a 7.4 kW wall box covers most daily driving.

Then check what came with the car: often a portable charger, sometimes a wall box that the car maker arranges to install. For an electric scooter, follow the maker's instructions for its charger and socket, or see [2W and 3W chargers](/chargers/2w-3w-chargers/).

## Step 2: Read your sanctioned load and phase

Your electricity bill shows your sanctioned load, in kW or kVA, and whether the connection is single or three phase. A 3.3 kW charger needs a 16 A single-phase circuit and a 7.4 kW charger draws 32 A on single phase, while 11 and 22 kW chargers draw 16 A and 32 A on each of three phases.

The charger runs for hours, on top of whatever else is on at the time: air conditioners, a geyser, the kitchen. Add them up for the hours you plan to charge. If the total comes close to your sanctioned load, you have three options:

- **A load increase** from your DISCOM.
- **A lower current setting** on the charger, which charges more slowly.
- **A wall box with load management**, which slows charging when the house needs the power.

With three-phase supply, a single-phase charger goes on the phase with the least load, to keep the phases balanced.

## Step 3: Choose where to mount the charger

- **Near the charging port.** Park as usual and mount the charger where its cable reaches the charging port with some slack, without crossing a walkway.
- **At the right height.** The maker's manual gives the mounting height and the space to leave around the unit.
- **Out of the rain where you can.** In open parking, check the charger's IP rating, add a hood or canopy if the spot takes direct rain, and avoid places where water collects.
- **Out of harm's way.** Keep it clear of gates, doors and reversing cars, or protect it with a wheel stop.
- **With room for the DB box** beside it, and mobile or Wi-Fi signal if the charger uses an app.

## Step 4: Plan the cable route

The charger needs its own circuit: a dedicated cable from your meter or main DB, shared with no sockets or lights, sized for full current for hours with little voltage drop. A longer run needs a thicker cable.

| Charger | Supply | Typical cable, run up to 30 m | Longer run | Breaker |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.3 kW AC | Single phase, 16 A | 2.5–4 sq mm copper | 6 sq mm copper | 20 A MCB |
| 7.4 kW AC | Single phase, 32 A | 6 sq mm copper | 10 sq mm copper | 40 A MCB |
| 11 kW AC | Three phase, 16 A | 4 sq mm copper, 5-core | 6 sq mm copper, 5-core | 20 A four-pole MCB |
| 22 kW AC | Three phase, 32 A | 6–10 sq mm copper, 5-core | 16 sq mm copper, 5-core | 40 A four-pole MCB |

These are typical starting points, the same ones our installation checklist uses; the charger maker's manual and your actual route set the final size and breaker. The route depends on your home:

- **Houses and villas.** Along the wall to the porch or garage, in conduit or clamped out of reach of cars and gates; across a driveway, underground as armoured cable in a pipe.
- **Housing societies.** From your meter in the meter room along the route the society allows, on cable tray or in conduit along the basement ceiling, clear of vehicle height.

## Step 5: Fit a DB box with the right RCD

The charger gets a small distribution box of its own, close to it, with:

- **An MCB** sized for the charger and the cable, as in the table above.
- **The RCD the charger maker asks for**, usually a Type A RCD with 6 mA DC fault detection, or a Type B RCD. The common Type AC RCD does not detect the DC fault current a charger can produce, and fitting the wrong type is a common mistake. Some wall boxes have DC detection built in, which changes what the DB box needs, so follow the manual.
- **A surge protection device (SPD)** against voltage spikes.

See [DB boxes for EV chargers](/services/ev-charger-db-box/) for what goes inside one.

## Step 6: Make a dedicated, tested earth

Most charger makers ask for a dedicated earth with a low, tested resistance. That usually means a new earth pit near the charger, pipe or chemical depending on the soil and space. The work should follow the maker's manual, the Central Electricity Authority's safety regulations, IS 3043 for earthing and IS 732 for wiring.

Ask your installer to measure the earth resistance and the neutral-to-earth voltage and write both down. Where a pit can't be dug near a basement bay, a tested earth can be brought in from outside, or the building's earthing used if the charger maker allows it. See [earthing for EV chargers](/services/ev-charger-earthing/).

## Step 7: Get your society's permission

If the charger goes in common parking, or the cable runs through common areas, most societies want a written request covering:

- the charger model and its power
- the cable route from your meter to your bay
- how the electricity is metered and paid for
- the safety measures: dedicated circuit, RCD and earthing

Get the permission in writing before work starts. If your society is planning shared chargers for many residents, see [housing societies](/for/housing-societies/).

## Step 8: Decide on a separate EV connection

The central government's charging guidelines let home owners charge on their existing connection or take a separate metered connection from their DISCOM with a dedicated EV charging tariff ([Lok Sabha answer, Dec 2024](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/2985666/1/AS254_WdMGkS.pdf)). Whether your DISCOM offers one, at what tariff and with what paperwork, varies, so ask yours.

It can make sense when your home's load is already high, or in a society that bills the charger's power apart from your flat. If you apply, do it early: the charger can be switched on only once the new meter is in.

## Step 9: Installation day

Once the materials are ready and the route agreed, a typical wall box usually takes a few hours on site:

1. The supply is switched off and the circuit isolated before anyone works on it.
2. The cable is laid along the agreed route, in conduit, on tray or in a trench.
3. The DB box is fixed and wired, and the earth is made and connected.
4. The charger is mounted at the height the manual gives, with a holder for the gun and its cable.

## Step 10: Test it and charge for the first time

The charger should be tested before it is handed over, not just switched on:

- **Insulation resistance** of the new cable.
- **Earth resistance** and **neutral-to-earth voltage**.
- **An RCD trip test**, to show it cuts the supply when it should.
- **App setup**, with a lower current limit if your load is tight.
- **A test charge with your car**, to check that charging starts at the power you expect.

Ask for a handover pack with the test results, photos of the work and the charger's warranty details.

## Safety: do's and don'ts

**Do:**

- Charge only from a dedicated circuit with its own MCB and RCD.
- Dock the gun after every charge, and keep the cable off the floor and away from the wheels.
- Check the cable and plug now and then, and stop using them if they are cut, crushed or scorched.
- Call an electrician if the RCD trips more than once.

**Don't:**

- Use extension boards, multi-plug adapters or extension cords, even for a portable charger.
- Plug a car's portable charger into an ordinary 6 A socket, or into a 16 A socket that shares its circuit with other appliances, for long charging.
- Run a charging cable through a window or door, or across a driveway where a car can crush it.
- Bypass an RCD that keeps tripping, or let anyone untrained open the charger or DB box.
