## What DC fast charger installation involves

A DC fast charger needs a three-phase supply, a panel, heavy cable and a solid base, all sized before the charger arrives. We take care of the whole path, from your meter or transformer to the charging gun:

- **Power check.** Your connection type and sanctioned load or contract demand, the space in your existing panel, and the load your site will need when more chargers come.
- **LT panel.** An incomer, a breaker for each charger with the residual-current or earth-fault protection the maker specifies, surge protection, and a meter for each charger if you want energy tracked separately.
- **Armoured cable and trench.** Copper or aluminium armoured cable sized for the input current and the length of the run, laid in a trench on a sand bed under a protective cover and warning tape, or pulled through pipe where it crosses a driveway.
- **Foundation and plinth.** A concrete plinth raised above the site's flood level, with anchor bolts set to the maker's template and cable-entry pipes cast in.
- **Earthing.** Dedicated earth pits, tested for resistance and bonded the way the maker's manual asks. See [earthing](/services/ev-charger-earthing/).
- **Commissioning.** Electrical tests, power-up, the OCPP connection to your CMS and a test charge.

## How much power each charger draws

Size the supply from the charger's input, not its output. No charger is 100 per cent efficient and the supply voltage varies, so a charger draws a little more current than its output alone suggests.

| Charger output | Input current per phase at 415 V (roughly) | Supply it usually needs |
|---|---|---|
| 30 kW | 45–50 A | Three-phase LT |
| 60 kW | 90–100 A | Three-phase LT |
| 120 kW | 180–200 A | LT if the site's total load is within the limit, otherwise HT |
| 180 kW | 270–300 A | Usually HT with a transformer |
| 240 kW | 360–400 A | Usually HT with a transformer |

These figures are for planning. The charger's datasheet gives its exact maximum input current, and we size the cable, breaker and sanctioned load from that, with the rest of the site's load added.

Plan for the next charger now. A trench with a spare pipe and a panel with a spare outgoing breaker are far easier to build in one go than to dig up and rebuild later.

## LT or HT: the connection shapes the design

The central government's charging guidelines let charging station owners opt for an LT connection for loads up to 150 kW ([Lok Sabha answer, December 2024](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/2985666/1/AS254_WdMGkS.pdf)). Within that, a 30 or 60 kW charger, two 60 kW chargers or one 120 kW unit can usually run on a three-phase LT supply with an LT panel. The limit applies to the whole connection, so the site's lights, canopy and other loads count too.

Above it, or where your state's rules set a different limit, the site needs an HT connection, usually at 11 kV, with its own transformer. That adds HT switchgear, the DISCOM's metering, and space, a plinth, fencing and earthing for the transformer, so it changes your layout and your timeline. We size the LT side from the transformer onwards and tell you what the HT side needs, such as the transformer rating and where it can stand.

How each state and DISCOM applies the LT option varies, so we check the current rule for your site before the design is fixed. The connection is taken in the site owner's name; we tell you the load to apply for and what to prepare.

## Foundation, bays and protection

- **Plinth height.** The cabinet sits high enough that monsoon water never reaches its cable entry.
- **Clearances.** DC chargers have fans and service doors, so we leave the space the maker asks for around the cabinet, for airflow and for opening it.
- **Bollards or wheel stops** in front of the cabinet, so a reversing car can't hit it.
- **Cable management.** Gun holsters and cable hangers keep the heavy charging cable off the ground, where it could be driven over.
- **Bay layout.** Each gun reaches its car's charging port comfortably, with lighting and floor marking for use at night.

For canopies, paving and larger layouts, see [civil work](/services/ev-charging-civil-work/).

## Dual-gun chargers and power sharing

Many DC chargers from 60 kW up have two guns. With one car plugged in, the charger can give it up to its full output; with two, it shares its power between them. A 120 kW dual-gun charger can give up to 120 kW to one car, or split it, for example 60 kW each, when two are connected. Each car also takes only as much as its own battery system accepts, which for many cars is well below the charger's maximum.

For the installation, two things follow: the supply and cable are sized for the charger's full input, however the power is split, and the bays are laid out so each gun reaches its car without strain.

## Commissioning and the OCPP connection

Once the power is on, we commission the charger: insulation and earth tests, protection checks, phase sequence and the emergency stop, then configuration. Most networked chargers talk to their charge management system (CMS) over OCPP, so we set the backend URL and charge point ID, update the firmware if the maker or operator asks, and run a test charge to check that the session, energy and status reach your CMS. If you don't run a CMS yet, [Proline CMS](/cms/) is our own. The full checklist is on our [commissioning](/services/ev-charger-commissioning/) page.
