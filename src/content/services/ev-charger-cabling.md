## What our cabling work covers

- **Size the cable** for the charger's current, the length of the run and the way it is laid, following the charger maker's manual and IS 732, the Indian code for wiring installations.
- **Supply it:** copper or aluminium, flexible or armoured, single phase or three phase, cut to your route.
- **Lay it** clamped on walls, in conduit, on cable tray, or underground in a trench or pipe.
- **Terminate it** with glands, crimped lugs, sleeves and labels at both ends.
- **Test it** for insulation resistance and continuity before the charger is powered, as part of commissioning.

## Typical cable sizes for EV chargers

These are typical starting points for short runs, under about 30 metres. They are not a design: the survey measures your route, and the factors in the next section can push the size up.

| Charger | Current it draws | Typical cable for a short run |
|---|---|---|
| 3.3 kW AC | 16 A, single phase | 2.5–4 sq mm copper, 3-core |
| 7.4 kW AC | 32 A, single phase | 6 sq mm copper, 3-core (10 sq mm for longer runs) |
| 11 kW AC | 16 A, three phase | 4 sq mm copper, 5-core |
| 22 kW AC | 32 A, three phase | 6–10 sq mm copper, 5-core |
| 30 kW DC | About 50 A, three phase | 16 sq mm copper or 25 sq mm aluminium, armoured |
| 60 kW DC | About 90–100 A, three phase | 35 sq mm copper or 50–70 sq mm aluminium, armoured |
| 120 kW DC | About 180–200 A, three phase | 95 sq mm copper or 150 sq mm aluminium, armoured |
| 240 kW DC | About 350–400 A, three phase | 240 sq mm copper, or parallel runs, as designed |

Chargers of 120 kW and more are usually fed from their own LT panel, or from a transformer on an HT connection, and the cable is designed with that supply. See [DC fast charger installation](/services/dc-fast-charger-installation/).

## What changes the cable size

- **Continuous load.** A charger draws its full current for hours, unlike most household appliances, which run in short bursts or switch on and off. We size the cable for that full current, not an average.
- **Run length and voltage drop.** Every metre of cable loses a little voltage. Over a long run the charger sees less voltage, may slow down or stop with an under-voltage error, and the cable runs warmer. For example, a 7.4 kW charger draws 32 A: over a 50-metre run, 6 sq mm copper loses about 5% of the voltage, and 10 sq mm about 3%.
- **How the cable is laid.** Cable clipped in open air sheds heat better than cable in conduit, buried in the ground or closed inside a wall, so the same cable carries less current on some routes.
- **Grouping.** Cables bunched on one tray or in one trench heat each other, so each one is derated. This matters on sites with several chargers.
- **Ambient temperature.** Indian summers, sun-baked roofs and hot basements cut what a cable can carry. We size for the hottest conditions the route will see.

## Copper or aluminium?

**Copper** carries more current for its size, bends more easily and suits charger terminals. We use it for AC chargers and most short runs, and wherever the charger maker asks for it.

**Aluminium** is lighter and lower in material cost, so it is common for long, heavy feeds to DC chargers from an LT panel. It needs a larger size for the same current, as the table shows, bimetallic lugs where it meets copper terminals or busbars, and careful termination, because a poorly made aluminium joint can heat up over time.

## Underground runs: armoured cable, trenches and road crossings

Cable that is buried, or runs where it can be crushed or hit, should be armoured. The steel armour protects the cores and is earthed through the cable glands. For a buried run:

- The trench is dug to the depth the design calls for, the cable is laid on a bed of sand and covered with protective bricks or tiles, and warning tape goes in above it.
- Where the cable may need to be pulled out or replaced later, it runs in HDPE or DWC pipe. If you plan more chargers, lay a spare pipe now; it saves digging up the same ground twice.
- Driveways and internal roads are crossed in a pipe sleeve, so the cable can be replaced without breaking the road again.
- The surface is restored: concrete, tiles, paver blocks or tar.

Crossing a public road or footpath needs permission from the authority that owns it, and we tell you what to ask for. Trenching and road crossings are part of our [civil work](/services/ev-charging-civil-work/).

## Conduit, tray and basement routes

Inside a building, cable runs in PVC or GI conduit or on cable tray, fixed at regular spacing and kept away from water pipes and drains. Basement parking, where many society chargers go, needs extra care:

- Cable runs high on walls or ceilings, on tray or in conduit, clear of vehicles and ramps.
- Fire-retardant, low-smoke (FRLS) cable is used where the building or the society asks for it, and holes through fire-rated walls and floors are sealed after the cable passes.
- Joints and boxes stay above the level water reaches when the basement floods.
- The route is agreed with the society before work starts; most ask for an NOC. See [housing societies](/for/housing-societies/).

In a society, the cable often starts at the flat's meter in the meter room and runs to the parking slot, so the run can be long. That is why we measure it at the survey instead of guessing.

## Glands, terminations and labels

Many cable faults start at the ends, not in the middle of the run. So we:

- fit double-compression glands on armoured cable, with the armour earthed, and weatherproof glands where the cable enters an outdoor charger or box
- crimp lugs with the correct die, use bimetallic lugs for aluminium, and fit pin lugs or ferrules on fine-stranded conductors
- tighten every terminal to the torque its maker gives
- sleeve and label both ends, so the next electrician knows what feeds what

Then the cable is connected to the [DB box](/services/ev-charger-db-box/) and the [earth](/services/ev-charger-earthing/), and the run is tested before power-up.
