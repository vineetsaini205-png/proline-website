## Why an EV charger needs its own earth

While a car charges, its metal body is connected to the charger's earth through the charging cable. If a fault makes a metal part live, the earth gives the fault current a safe path, so the protection in the [DB box](/services/ev-charger-db-box/) trips before anyone gets a shock. That makes the earth one of the most important parts of the installation, and one of the easiest to get wrong.

- **It clears faults.** Without a sound earth, a fault can leave the charger's body, or the car, at a dangerous voltage.
- **The charger checks it.** Many chargers test the earth before and during a charge. A poor earth shows up as earth fault errors, failed starts or sessions that stop midway.
- **Surge protection needs it.** A surge protection device can only send a lightning or switching surge to ground if it has a low-resistance path to earth.
- **Old earths are often weak.** A building's earth may be years old, dry, corroded or shared with pumps, lifts and inverters. We test it rather than assume it is fine.

Most charger makers ask for a dedicated earth for the charger, and that is what we build. How the new pit ties in with your building's existing earthing is part of the design, not an afterthought.

## Pipe earthing or chemical earthing

Both types work when they are designed for the soil and tested. The difference is how they behave over the years.

| | Pipe earthing (GI or copper) | Chemical (maintenance-free) earthing |
|---|---|---|
| How it is made | A GI or copper pipe electrode in a dug pit, traditionally backfilled with soil, charcoal and salt, with a funnel for watering | A copper-bonded or GI electrode in a bored hole, packed with a conductive backfill compound that holds moisture |
| Life | GI corrodes, faster in salty or acidic soil, and the salt in a traditional pit speeds this up. Copper lasts much longer | Long, because the electrode is protected and the compound does not wash away. The maker states the design life |
| Maintenance | Water poured in during dry months, backfill checked, a yearly test | No watering, a yearly test |
| Resistance through the year | Rises in the dry season as the soil dries out | Steadier, because the compound keeps the electrode in moist, conductive contact |
| Soil it suits | Moist, loamy or clay soil with a reasonable water table | Dry, sandy or rocky ground and hill sites, as well as ordinary soil |
| Where it suits | Homes and open sites with soft soil, where the pit can be reached and watered | DC chargers, public and commercial sites, paved or tight spaces, and anywhere a steady value matters |

On commercial and public sites, chemical earthing is usually the better choice, because it holds a steadier value with no watering. At a home with soft, moist soil, a well-made pipe earth does the job. Either way, the test result decides whether the pit passes, not the type written on the quote.

## How we build an earth pit

1. **Check the soil and the existing earth.** At the [site survey](/services/site-survey/) we test the earth already there and look at the ground. On larger sites, a soil resistivity test (the Wenner four-pin method) tells us how deep to go and how many electrodes are needed.
2. **Pick the spot.** Close to the charger and DB box to keep the earth conductor short, in open soil, and clear of foundations, water tanks and buried services. For basement chargers, the pit goes outside, where there is soil.
3. **Dig or bore, and place the electrode.** To the depth the design calls for, with the backfill that suits the type of earth and the soil.
4. **Build the chamber.** A brick or precast chamber with a cover, so the connection can be seen and tested later without digging.
5. **Run the earth conductor.** GI or copper strip or wire, sized for the fault current, to the earth bar in the DB box and on to the charger, with crimped lugs and tight, protected joints.
6. **Test and record.** Every pit is tested before the charger is powered, as below.

We size the electrode and conductors to IS 3043, the Indian Standard code of practice for earthing, and to the charger maker's manual, and our work follows the Central Electricity Authority's safety regulations.

## How earth resistance is tested

We test each new earth pit with an earth resistance tester, using the three-point fall-of-potential method:

- The pit is disconnected at its test link, so the tester measures this electrode alone and not every earth path in the building.
- Two test spikes go into the ground in a straight line from the pit: a current spike well away from it, and a potential spike at about 62% of that distance.
- The tester passes a small current through the ground between the pit and the far spike, and reads the resistance in ohms.
- We move the potential spike a little nearer and a little farther and test again. If the readings agree, the value holds. If they do not, the spikes go farther out and we repeat.

We record the value, the date, the method and whether the ground was dry or wet. A reading taken in the dry season matters most, because that is when resistance is highest. A clamp-on tester is handy for a quick check of a connected system with several earth paths, but it does not replace a three-point test for a new pit.

## What earth resistance does an EV charger need?

There is no single figure that is right for every charger. The target comes from two places: the charger maker's installation manual, which often states a maximum, and the earthing design for your site, which depends on your supply system and the protection it relies on. We work to the stricter of the two and write the target and the measured value side by side in the handover pack, so you can see that the pit passes.

If a reading is too high, the fix is in the ground: a deeper electrode, a second electrode connected in parallel, or better backfill.

## Neutral-to-earth voltage checks

Low earth resistance is half the story. At [commissioning](/services/ev-charger-commissioning/) we also measure the voltage between neutral and earth at the charger, with no load and again during a test charge. Many chargers watch this voltage and refuse to start, or stop during a session, when it goes above the limit in their manual.

A high neutral-to-earth voltage usually points to:

- a weak or corroded earth, or a loose earth joint
- a loose, undersized or shared neutral in the building's wiring
- heavy, unbalanced load across the three phases of the supply

Never accept the quick fix of linking neutral to earth at the charger to make the error go away. It hides the fault and can put a voltage on the charger's body and the car. The cause needs to be traced and put right.
