## Which home charger: 7.4, 11 or 22 kW

Your car decides the charging speed more than the wall box does. Every electric car has an on-board charger that limits how much AC power it takes, and most cars sold in India accept 7.2 kW, some 11 kW. A bigger wall box than your car can use needs more supply and does not charge any faster.

| Charger | Current | Supply it needs | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.3 kW socket point or portable charger | 16 A, single phase | Any home connection with spare load | Short daily drives, charged overnight |
| 7.4 kW wall box | 32 A, single phase | Single or three phase, with enough sanctioned load | Most cars, overnight or in a few hours |
| 11 kW wall box | 16 A per phase | Three phase | Cars that accept 11 kW on AC |
| 22 kW wall box | 32 A per phase | Three phase, with a high sanctioned load | Several cars sharing one charger; few cars take the full 22 kW |

As a rough guide, charging time is the battery size divided by the power your car takes, plus about 10 per cent for losses: a 30 kWh battery takes about four and a half hours from empty at 7.2 kW. Need help choosing? See [AC and home chargers](/chargers/ac-chargers/); our advice on any brand is free.

## Single phase or three phase

A 7.4 kW charger runs on single phase, so it suits most homes. It draws 32 A for hours at a stretch, on top of your air conditioners, geyser and kitchen, so the real question is how much of your sanctioned load is free when you charge. We read the load from your electricity bill and ask what else runs at night.

If your home has three-phase supply, a 7.4 kW charger goes on the phase with the least load, to keep the phases balanced. 11 and 22 kW chargers need three-phase supply.

If the load is tight, there are three ways out: a load increase from your DISCOM, a lower current setting on the charger, or a wall box with load management, which reads your home's total load through a sensor and slows charging when the house needs the power.

## What a safe home installation needs

- **A dedicated circuit.** The charger gets its own cable from your meter or main DB and never shares a circuit with sockets or lights.
- **A DB box at the charger.** An MCB, the RCD your charger's manual asks for (often Type A with 6 mA DC detection, or Type B) and surge protection. See [DB boxes](/services/ev-charger-db-box/).
- **A tested earth.** Most makers ask for a dedicated earth with a low resistance. We test the earth you have and make a new pit when it falls short. See [earthing](/services/ev-charger-earthing/).
- **Cable sized for hours of full load.** For a 7.4 kW charger this is usually 6 sq mm copper on a short run and 10 sq mm on a long one, so the voltage drop stays small.
- **The right mounting.** On the wall at the height the manual gives, close enough that the cable reaches your car's charging port without lying across the floor. Where there is no wall, a pedestal.
- **Cover in open parking.** We check the charger's IP rating and add a hood or canopy if the spot takes direct rain.

## Houses, societies and basements

**Independent houses and villas.** The cable usually runs a short way from the meter or main DB, along the wall to the porch or garage, in conduit or clamped out of reach of cars and gates. Where it has to cross a driveway, it goes underground as armoured cable or in a pipe.

**Housing societies.** Your meter is often in a meter room on the ground floor or in the basement, and your parking bay can be a long way from it. The cable runs from your meter to your bay along the route the society allows. In basements it goes on cable tray or in conduit along the ceiling or walls, where vehicles can't hit it. If a new earth pit can't be dug near the bay, we bring a tested earth from outside, or use the building's earthing where the charger maker allows it.

**Society permission (NOC).** Most societies want a written request before a charger goes into common parking or a cable runs through common areas. It usually covers the charger model, the cable route, how the electricity is metered and paid for, and the safety measures. We give you these details for your application. If your society is planning shared chargers for many residents, see [housing societies](/for/housing-societies/).

## A separate EV meter or connection

The central government's charging guidelines let home owners charge on their existing connection or take a separate metered connection from their DISCOM with a dedicated EV tariff ([Lok Sabha answer, December 2024](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/2985666/1/AS254_WdMGkS.pdf)). Whether your DISCOM offers this, and its tariff and paperwork, vary by DISCOM, so check with yours before you decide. We tell you what to prepare, and connect the charger to the new meter once it is in.

A separate connection can make sense when your home's load is already high, or in a society where you want the charger's power billed apart from your flat. Otherwise, charging on your existing connection is simpler.

## The charger that came with your car, or one you are moving

Many electric cars in India come with a wall box, and the car maker often arranges a standard installation. If yours isn't covered, or your home needs more than the standard fit, such as a longer cable run, a new earth pit or a basement route, we install it. Share the model and its manual and we follow the maker's requirements.

Moving house, or moving the charger to a better spot? We take it down, make the old circuit safe, and install it at the new place with the cable, DB box and earthing it needs there. Then we test it as a new installation. Check your warranty first: some makers want their own technician to move a charger.
