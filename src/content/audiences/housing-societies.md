## What residents and RWAs ask

Questions about charging in a society come from two sides:

- **Residents:** My bay is far from my meter; can a charger reach it? What does the committee need before it gives the NOC?
- **RWAs and managing committees:** If many residents install chargers, will our supply trip? How do we bill fairly for a shared charger? Is charging safe in our basement?

Both want the same thing: chargers that are safe, paid for fairly and within what the building's supply can carry.

## Private chargers or shared chargers

| | Private charger at a resident's bay | Shared chargers run by the society |
|---|---|---|
| Power comes from | The resident's own meter, or a separate sub-meter | The common connection with a meter on each charger, or a separate EV connection |
| Who pays | The resident, on their own bill | Each user, by meter reading, RFID card or app |
| Suits | Allotted bays with a workable cable route from the meter | Unallotted bays, a distant meter room, or many EVs |
| Visitors | Not usually | Can be allowed |
| Cable work | One cable run per charger, from each meter | One supply to a group of chargers |

The Ministry of Power's 2024 charging guidelines make room for both ([guidelines, Sep 2024](https://beeindia.gov.in/WriteReadData/L45218/2841453721548450.pdf)):

- Residents can install private chargers in their own designated parking, supplied through their existing meter or a separate sub-meter, as they choose.
- RWAs can set up community charging in the society. Residents decide the type and number of chargers, and visitors can be allowed to charge.
- Residents, RWAs and societies can ask their DISCOM for a separate metered connection with a dedicated EV tariff.
- The society sets the fee for community charging, based on the electricity tariff that applies.

A private charger on a resident's own meter is billed at domestic rates on that resident's bill, which keeps the society out of billing altogether. For one resident's charger, see [home EV charger installation](/services/home-ev-charger-installation/).

## Load management: sharing the society's power

A 7.4 kW charger draws 32 A for hours. Ten of them charging at 9 pm, while lifts, pumps and air conditioners run, add about 74 kW to the building's evening peak. There are four ways to handle it:

- **Load management.** Many networked AC chargers can share a set power limit. Ten chargers sharing 30 kW, for example, slow down when many cars are plugged in and speed up as cars finish; a car parked overnight still gets far more than a day's city driving.
- **A reading of the building's load.** Some systems also measure the building's total load and lower the charging limit when the building needs the power.
- **Smaller chargers.** A 3.3 kW point adds about 25–30 kWh over a nine-hour night, enough for most daily driving, on far less supply.
- **A load increase.** If the supply is still short, the society applies to its DISCOM for a higher sanctioned load, as the guidelines provide. We work out how much to ask for.

## Billing residents fairly

Agree the billing method before the first charge. Three ways work:

- **A meter on each charger.** Works with any charger: the meters are read each month and the energy is added to each user's maintenance bill. It suits a few chargers with regular users.
- **RFID cards or app login.** Networked chargers record every session against a card or account, so the society knows who used what without reading meters.
- **Proline CMS.** Our charge management software runs OCPP chargers of any brand. Residents join the society's group by mobile number, email or RFID card and get a members' price or members-only chargers; other drivers pay by UPI, and unused money is refunded automatically. The society can run it on its own server, or we host it on servers in India with 24/7 support for the CMS, and there is no commission on charging payments. See [Proline CMS](/cms/).

Put the rules in writing at the general body: the price per unit, who pays for the chargers (society funds or the users), and how long a car may stay in a charging bay once it is full.

## The NOC and the RWA's role

For a private charger, the resident applies to the society; for shared chargers, the managing committee usually takes a proposal to the general body. Either way, the request normally covers:

- The charger model and its power
- The cable route from the meter to the bay, and how the cable is fixed and protected
- How the electricity is metered and paid for
- The safety measures: DB box, RCD, earthing and an isolator
- Who services the charger if it develops a fault

We give residents and committees these technical details in writing for the application.

Rules differ by state. In Maharashtra, a 2022 circular of the co-operative registrar asks societies to issue the NOC in seven days when the installation follows the Chief Electrical Inspector's safety advisory ([Cyril Amarchand Mangaldas, Feb 2025](https://conventuslaw.com/report/india-can-housing-societies-withhold-permission-to-install-electric-vehicle-chargers/)). Delhi's EV Policy 2026 encourages housing societies and RWAs to facilitate community and private charging ([Daily Pioneer, Jul 2026](https://www.dailypioneer.com/news/slug-lite/delhi-notifies-ev-policy-2026-subsidies-within-60-days?year=2026)).

## Basements: safe charging underground

Check the fire rules first. Maharashtra's fire service, for example, says in its July 2024 parking guidelines that EV charging is not to be provided in basement, puzzle or automated parking, and that charging areas must be ventilated ([Maharashtra Fire Service circular, 18 Jul 2024](https://mahafireservice.gov.in/circular/Guidelines%20of%20Parking.pdf)). The Ministry of Power's list of documents for a charging connection also includes a fire department NOC when a charger goes in a basement. Ask your local fire authority what applies to your building, and what the charging area needs, such as extinguishers or detectors.

Where basement charging is allowed, we install it with care:

- **Cable on metal tray or in conduit** along the ceiling or walls, above vehicle height.
- **A DB box for each charger** with an MCB, the RCD the maker asks for and surge protection, plus an isolator near the bays so the supply can be cut in an emergency.
- **A tested earth**, brought from outside if no pit can be dug near the bays.
- **Chargers clear of ramps, exits and fire equipment**, with wheel stops or bollards where cars could hit them.
- **A network that works underground**, such as Ethernet from the floor above, for chargers that bill through software.
- **Signs** marking the charging bays and the isolator.
